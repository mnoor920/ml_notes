Introduction to Data ScienceLec 1 · 19 slides

Definition: the study of data to derive useful insight for decision making. It combines mathematics and statistics (analysis), computer science (computation and algorithms) and domain expertise (problem understanding). It predicts future outcomes, not only describes the past. Examples: Netflix recommendations, hospital risk prediction, bank fraud detection.

Why data science

Why it exists

Explosion of data (social media, sensors, IoT) · evidence-based decisions instead of intuition · innovation (self-driving cars, personalised medicine) · competitive advantage.

Why it is important

Better decisions · efficiency · personalised experiences · predicting the future · drives innovation · benefits society (health, education, transport).

How it works (nine steps across slides 5–6)

Collection→Cleaning→Analysis→Visualisation→Decision-making→Modelling→Evaluation→Deployment→Monitoring & feedback

The Data Science LifecycleLec 2 · 25 slides · running example: e-commerce churn

1 Problem formulation→2 Collection→3 Cleaning→4 EDA→5 Modelling→6 Deployment→7 Monitoring→8 Feedback loop↺

The cycle is circular and iterative, not linear: a drop in accuracy (monitoring) can reveal changed behaviour (feedback) and send you back to problem formulation or collection.

StageKey ideaChurn example
1 Problem formulationMost critical stage. Statement must be SMART. Output: actionable problem + success metricsCut churn 10% in 6 months; deliver in 3 months
2 CollectionSources: internal DBs (CRM), external/public data, APIs, web scraping. Mind privacy and GDPRDemographics, purchase history (RFM), web interactions, support contacts
3 CleaningUp to 80% of effort. Missing values, formats, types, outliers, duplicates10% missing age → median; two date formats; "−$500"
4 EDASummaries, visualisation, correlation. Output: insights and hypothesesLow purchase frequency and many support tickets → more churn
5 ModellingFeature engineering, train/test split, algorithm selection, training and evaluationRandom Forest, 80/20 split, 85% accuracy, 75% precision
6 DeploymentBatch (scheduled scoring) or API endpoint (real time). Challenges: integration, scalability, latency, infrastructureREST API scores user at login; high risk → 10% discount
7 MonitoringModels degrade through concept / data drift. Watch performance, input drift, prediction driftAlert if accuracy < 80% or new-customer age shifts
8 Feedback loopOutcomes of actions feed back into the data; model retrained periodicallyDid customer A stay after the offer? Add to data, retrain

SMART

Specific · Measurable · Achievable · Relevant · Time-bound. Key questions: business objective, desired outcome, success measure, constraints (time, resources, data).

Data types by structure

Structured: rows and columns (SQL, transactions). Semi-structured: some organisation, not relational (JSON, XML). Unstructured: no predefined format (reviews, emails, images).

The human element

Business stakeholders (problem and metrics) · data engineers (pipelines) · data scientists (models) · IT/DevOps (deployment and monitoring). Ethics, fairness and bias must be considered at every stage.

Exam tipThree drift terms are easy to confuse. Concept drift: relationship between inputs and target changes. Input (data) drift: distribution of incoming features changes. Prediction drift: distribution of model outputs changes.

Ethics & Responsible AILec 3 · 16 slides

Why ethics matter

Discrimination risks (biased algorithms disadvantage groups) · privacy breaches (identity theft, fraud) · loss of trust (resistance to beneficial AI).

Three sources of bias

Skewed training data

Historical data reflects past discrimination; e.g. biased hiring history produces a biased hiring model.

Flawed algorithm design

Objectives, feature selection, architecture and metrics can favour some groups.

Human prejudices

Developers' unconscious biases shape problem framing and evaluation.

Case study: facial recognition (Joy Buolamwini, MIT)

Error up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women vs 0.8% for lighter-skinned men. Cause: training sets mostly lighter-skinned faces plus inadequate testing across groups. Harm: false identification, wrongful arrests, exclusion.

Accountability and transparency

Opacity problem: deep models are hard to interpret, so decisions cannot be audited or challenged. High-stakes decisions (justice, healthcare, finance) make this a due-process issue.

Privacy laws

LawScopeKey points
GDPR (EU, 2018)Most comprehensive globallyExplicit consent · right to access, correct, delete ("right to be forgotten") · privacy by design · impact assessments for high-risk processing · fine up to 4% of global annual revenue or €20m, whichever is higher
HIPAA (US)Protected health information (PHI)Strict PHI access controls · business associate agreements · electronic PHI safeguards · patient access rights
PDPA (Singapore)Private-sector personal dataConsent · purpose limitation · breach notification · access and correction rights

Five Responsible AI principles

PrincipleMeaning
FairnessTreat all individuals and groups equitably
TransparencyDecisions understandable and auditable
AccountabilityClear responsibility and mechanisms to fix errors and harm
PrivacyPersonal data protected across the lifecycle; individuals keep control
SecurityRobust against attacks, manipulation and failures

Real-world dilemmas

  • Human in the loop: AI diagnosis and autonomous vehicles; who is morally responsible? Ambulance allocation could under-serve neighbourhoods if data is biased.
  • Privacy vs performance: more personal data improves models (COVID contact tracing, recommenders). Mitigations: differential privacy, federated learning.
  • Engagement vs well-being: social media algorithms amplify divisive content; business metrics vs societal good.

Tools and strategies

Ethical sourcing: representative data, consent, data lineage, bias audits, governance policies (retention, access control). Explainable AI (XAI): interpretability methods and attention; surrogate models (simple model approximating a black box) balance performance and explainability.

Data CollectionWeek 5 PDF · Part 1

Definition: the systematic process of gathering observations, measurements or records to answer a defined question. Every plan answers six questions: What (variables) · Why (question served) · Who/what (source) · How (method) · When (period, frequency) · Protected how (quality, privacy, storage).

Workflow: define question → list variables → choose source, method, sample → pilot and collect → store raw copy securely → write the data dictionary (name, meaning, type, unit, allowed range, source, how missing is coded).

Data Quality & Cleaning TechniquesWeek 5 PDF · Parts 2–3

Data cleaning: identifying and addressing data problems so the dataset is fit for its intended analysis. Golden rule: do not change every unusual value; every change needs a documented, defensible reason.

Why it matters: one typo 
Marks: 70, 80, 90, Missing, 800
Kept 800:        mean = (70+80+90+800)/4 = 260   median 85
800 → 80 typo:   mean = (70+80+90+80)/4  = 80    median 80
The missing value is silently ignored by both.

Seven dimensions of data quality

DimensionQuestionFailureCheck
AccuracyMatches reality?58 entered for 85Verify against source
CompletenessRequired data present?Attendance blankisnull().sum()
ConsistencyRecords agree?Two birth dates for one IDCross-table compare
ValidityObeys rules?Attendance 150%Range rules
UniquenessEach entity once?Order imported twiceduplicated()
TimelinessCurrent enough?Last year's inventoryTimestamps
IntegrityLinks hold?Order for unknown customerKey lookups
Accuracy vs validityA valid value obeys the rules (85 is within 0–100) but can still be inaccurate (the true mark was 58). A complete dataset can be inaccurate.

Ten-step cleaning workflow (order matters)

1 Back up raw→2 Inspect & profile→3 Remove duplicates→4 Fix types & formats→5 Standardise categories→6 Business rules→7 Investigate outliers→8 Missing values→9 Validate→10 Document

Why: duplicates go early so they do not distort the statistics used for imputation. Missing values go late because type conversion, invalid-value checks and deduplication create or reveal new missing values.

Technique 1 · Duplicates

KindExampleTreatment
ExactEvery column identicalDrop one
KeySame ID, different values (S5: 78 vs 87)Verify source, keep the correct one
Near"Muhammad Ali" vs "M. Ali"Standardise first, then re-check

Two orders by one customer for the same amount may be two real purchases: never delete just because some fields match. keep="first" | "last" | False (False flags all copies).

Technique 2 · Invalid values

Present and correctly typed but break a business rule: attendance 125%, marks 110, age −5, delivery before order, "Pass" with marks 12. Rules come from the data dictionary and domain experts. Flag, don't delete. Then decide: correct from source · set to missing · flag and keep · exclude with a written reason.

Technique 3 · Standardisation

"Lahore", "lahore", "LAHORE", " Lahore " → .str.strip().str.title(). Codes to labels with a documented mapping. Run value_counts() before and after; map() turns unmapped labels into NaN.

Technique 4 · Types and formats

"1,500" as text cannot be averaged; sorted text gives "100" < "25". Remove separators, then pd.to_numeric(errors="coerce"), pd.to_datetime(format="mixed", dayfirst=True, errors="coerce"). Coerce creates NaN/NaT, so inspect what failed.

Technique 5 · Outliers (IQR rule)

IQR = Q3 − Q1 Lower fence = Q1 − 1.5 × IQR Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR Z-score alternative: flag |z| > 3, z = (x − mean) / SD (weak on small samples)
Worked example (slides) 
Data: 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 180   (n = 7)
Q1 = 41.5   Q3 = 44.5   IQR = 3
Lower = 41.5 − 4.5 = 37     Upper = 44.5 + 4.5 = 49
180 > 49 → potential outlier
FindingExampleAction
Proven error800 for 80; grams vs kgCorrect; if impossible, set to missing
Genuine rare eventCEO salary, Eid sale spike, fraudKeep; use robust stats (median, IQR)
Cannot tellNo source, no expertFlag; report results with and without

Capping (winsorising): replace values beyond a fence with the fence value, s.clip(low, high). In fraud detection the outliers are the signal.

Missing ValuesWeek 5 PDF · Parts 4–5

Detected automatically: empty cell, NaN, None, NaT, pd.NA. Hidden: "N/A", "unknown", "?", "-", "", sentinels −999, 0, 99. A hidden −999 is worse than NaN because it silently enters the mean.

Missing ≠ zeroAttendance 0 means attended no classes. Attendance missing means we do not know.

Causes

Human error · technical failure · not applicable (fill with a label like "N/A") · non-response · data integration · data loss.

MCAR, MAR, MNAR

MeaningExampleTreatment
MCAR completely at randomUnrelated to any dataRandom disk error erases marksDeletion unbiased (loses data); simple imputation fine
MAR at randomDepends on other observed variablesAttendance missing more for one department (department recorded)Group-wise median, KNN
MNAR not at randomDepends on the missing value itselfWeak students don't submit marks; rich people hide incomeNo simple fix: domain knowledge, indicators, sensitivity analysis

MCAR: P(missing | data) = P(missing). You usually cannot prove the mechanism from the data alone. MAR check: df.groupby("department")["att_missing"].mean(); a big difference between groups suggests MAR.

How missing values distort statistics

Two slide examples 
Information loss: 10, 20, ?, 40 → observed mean 23.33 (true 25 with 30)
Bias (MNAR):      true 40, 55, 70, 85, 90 → mean 68
                  weakest two missing → 70, 85, 90 → mean 81.67  (overstated ≈ 14)
Compounding:      10 columns × 5% missing → 0.95¹⁰ ≈ 0.60 of rows complete

Decision workflow for a column

  1. Missing has a known meaning (no discount, N/A)? → constant / label
  2. Non-essential and mostly empty? → drop column
  3. Few rows, plausibly MCAR? → drop rows
  4. Ordered in time? → ffill / interpolate
  5. Otherwise → impute: median (skewed), mode (categorical), group-wise (MAR), KNN, plus indicator

Mean imputation shrinks variance

Worked example 
Data: 10, 20, ?, 30, 40     observed mean = 100/4 = 25 → fill 25
Squared deviations: 225 + 25 + 25 + 225 = 500 (the 25 adds 0)
Variance observed  = 500/4 = 125   SD 11.18
Variance imputed   = 500/5 = 100   SD 10.00   → looks less spread than it is

Median vs mean (skewed data)

Worked example 
Salaries (k PKR): 20, 25, 30, ?, 35, 100
Mean = 210/5 = 42   (higher than 4 of 5 people, pulled by 100)
Median = 30         → use median for skewed data (income, prices)

When mean and median differ a lot, the data is skewed: prefer the median.

Other methods

  • Mode (categorical): Python, SQL, Python, ?, Python, SQL → Python. Risk: over-represents top class; use "Unknown" if missing share is large. mode().iloc[0], document the tie-break.
  • Group-wise: CS 65, 70, 75, ? → 70; DS 80, 85, 90, ? → 85. Overall median 77.5 would be too high for CS and too low for DS. Small groups → fall back to overall median.
  • Missing indicator: create income_missing before imputing (otherwise all zeros). Useful under MNAR.
  • Constant: only when the constant is true (discount 0). A missing salary filled with 0 collapses the average.
  • Advanced: KNN (scale features first, slow) · regression / iterative (over-confident) · multiple imputation (best for inference). More complex is not automatically better.

There is no universal missing-percentage threshold that decides the treatment.

Data leakage

Leakage = information unavailable at prediction time influences training or evaluation.

Wrong: mean of ALL data→fill→split→score too optimistic
Right: split first→fit imputer on train→apply same value to test→honest score

Use a scikit-learn Pipeline (SimpleImputer → StandardScaler → model) so fit only sees training data. Never impute a feature using the target or future data.

Ten mistakes to avoid

Missing → zero · dropping every NaN row · deleting outliers without investigating · treating matching fields as duplicates · imputing before split · not checking what coerce made NaN · trusting a dataset because it is large · not documenting · imputing before deduplication · overwriting raw data.

EDA: Patterns, Trends, CorrelationWeek 7 · 16 slides

EDA means looking carefully at data with simple numbers and charts before using it: Look → Summarise → Ask. Like a doctor examining before prescribing.

Pattern

What is common, repeated or unusual. Common values (most score 70–90) · group differences (CS higher attendance) · repeating behaviour (shop busy every Friday) · unusual values (one score of 5).

Trend

General direction over time: upward (admissions), downward (old phone price), none (weekly attendance). Seasonality: pattern repeating at the same time each year (summer sales peak).

Correlation

Do two variables move together? Positive, negative or none. Measured by r from −1 to +1.

Interpreting r

|r|StrengthMeaning
below 0.3WeakLittle or no link
0.3 to 0.7ModerateClear but loose link
above 0.7StrongTight link (study hours vs marks r = 0.92)

Sign gives direction; size gives strength.

Pearson r = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) / √[ Σ(x − x̄)² · Σ(y − ȳ)² ] (formula not on the slides; included for numericals)
Correlation ≠ causationIce-cream sales and drowning rise together in summer. Hot weather (a confounder) causes both. Correlation shows a link, not the reason.

Four Python commands

df.describe() # summary of numeric columns df.groupby("dept")["marks"].mean() # compare groups → pattern df.plot(x="month", y="sales") # line chart → trend df.corr(numeric_only=True) # r for every pair

Case Study: Student Performance InvestigationModel answers to Q1–Q19

5,000 students, four departments (CS, DS, SE, EE). Your role: decide what to correct, remove, replace, investigate or keep.

Q1–Q2. Records vs survey; primary vs secondary.

University records: attendance, exam marks, department. Survey: daily study hours and satisfaction with teaching (not recorded anywhere). Records are secondary (collected for administration, reused); the survey is primary (collected for this study).

Q3–Q4. 200 CS students in the cafeteria.

Convenience sampling causing selection bias (only CS, only cafeteria-goers; three departments not represented). Better: stratified sampling: split the 5,000 by department and randomly sample from each in proportion to its size (e.g. if each department has 1,250, take 50 from each).

Q6. Cleaning order; why remove duplicates before imputation?

Back up → profile → remove duplicates → fix types ("unknown" → NaN) → standardise departments → check rules (S05, S06) → investigate outliers (S11) → handle missing → validate → document. The duplicate S08 would count twice in the mean/median, biasing the values used to fill gaps.

Q7–Q8. Duplicate S08; department labels.

Identical rows: keep one, log the removal. If marks differed it becomes a key duplicate: do not drop blindly, verify with the source to find the true record. Departments: standardisation (strip, upper-case, mapping Data Science → DS). Check value_counts() before to see every variant and after to confirm nothing became NaN or was wrongly merged.

Q9–Q11. S05 125%, S06 800 marks, S11 18 hours.

S05: invalid (breaks 0–100 rule) and also extreme, so both; but validity is the key point. Without the source, set to missing and flag (then impute if needed); do not guess 100. S06: do not assume 80 (could be 80 or 8, or 80.0). Check the source; if unavailable set to missing and flag. Do not remove the student, since their other fields are valid. S11: not automatically an error; 18 of 24 hours is implausible but possible during exams, or the respondent may have meant per week. Investigate the survey wording or contact the student. IQR may flag it but only as a screen; with small samples the decision needs domain rules, not IQR alone.

Q12. Method for each missing entry.

S04 study hours: group-wise (DS) median plus missing indicator. S07 attendance: retrieve from registry records first (it is a records field); otherwise SE median plus flag. S09 marks: do not impute: it is the outcome being analysed, so exclude S09 from marks analyses (listwise on that variable), keep the record otherwise. S10 "unknown": convert to NaN, then treat like S04 (EE group median + flag).

Q13–Q14. Income with 500; missing department.
Income: 40, 45, 50, 55, 60, 65, 500
Mean   = 815 / 7 = 116.4   (pulled up by 500)
Median = 55                → use median

500 may be genuine (a wealthy family), so keep it but use a robust statistic. For a missing department (categorical) use mode or better "Unknown", or simply look it up in university records. Risk of mode: over-represents the largest department and may misassign the student.

Q15. Group-wise imputation of study hours.
CS: 2, 3, 4 → mean 3        DS: 5, 6, 7 → mean 6
Overall mean = (2+3+4+5+6+7)/6 = 27/6 = 4.5
Group-wise: CS ? = 3, DS ? = 6

Use group-wise: study hours clearly depend on department (MAR). 4.5 would be too high for CS and too low for DS.

Q16–Q17. Sensor at 10:10; six-hour gap.

ffill 31 · bfill 33 · linear interpolation (31 + 33)/2 = 32. Recommend interpolation for a smoothly changing temperature. For a six-hour gap, interpolation draws a straight line that hides real daily variation; leave it missing and flag it, or use another sensor in the same lab. Avoid bfill if forecasting (uses the future).

Q18–Q19. Leakage; four readiness checks.

Computing the mean on all data lets test information leak into training, giving an over-optimistic score. Split first, fit the imputer on training data only, apply the training mean to the test set (a Pipeline enforces this). Checks: row count as expected · no NaN in key columns · unique IDs · values within valid ranges · categories valid (value_counts) · distributions before vs after · every change logged.

Final task. Is a dataset reliable just because it has no missing values or duplicates?

No. It can still be biased (convenience sample), inaccurate (valid-looking but wrong values such as 58 for 85), outdated, or distorted by careless imputation. Reliability needs representative collection, validation against sources and domain rules, and documentation.

Short answers

Q7. Define MCAR, MAR and MNAR with one example each.

MCAR: unrelated to any data (random disk error). MAR: depends on another observed variable (attendance missing more in one recorded department). MNAR: depends on the missing value itself (low scorers don't report marks).

Q8. Why does mean imputation reduce variance?

The filled value equals the mean, so it adds zero to the sum of squared deviations but increases n by one; variance falls and correlations weaken.

Q9. Write a SMART problem statement for reducing student dropout.

Identify first-year students at high risk of dropping out (S), reduce dropout by 15% (M) using existing attendance and grade records (A), protecting fee revenue and outcomes (R), within two semesters (T).

Q10. Name the eight lifecycle stages and say why the cycle is iterative.

Problem formulation, collection, cleaning, EDA, modelling, deployment, monitoring, feedback loop. Iterative because monitoring and feedback (e.g. drift) send you back to earlier stages.

Q11. List the five Responsible AI principles and three sources of bias.

Fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, security. Bias from skewed training data, flawed algorithm design, human prejudices.

Q12. Bank fraud data: should the single very large transaction be deleted? Are negative amounts errors?

No: in fraud detection the outlier may be the signal; verify, flag, keep. Negative amounts may be refunds; check business rules and transaction type before treating as invalid. Missing fraud labels: exclude those rows from training and evaluation (never impute the target).

Q13. Batch vs API deployment.

Batch: model runs on a schedule over many records (nightly churn scores). API: web service returns a prediction per request in real time (score at login). API needs low latency and scalability.

Q14. Key points of GDPR.

EU, 2018. Explicit consent; rights to access, correct and delete (right to be forgotten); privacy by design; impact assessments for high-risk processing; fines up to 4% of global annual revenue or €20m, whichever is higher.

Q15. Trend vs seasonality.

Trend is the long-term direction (sales rising year on year). Seasonality is a pattern repeating at the same time each period (sales peaking every summer).
